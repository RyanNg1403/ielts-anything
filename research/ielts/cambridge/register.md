# Language register in Cambridge Academic papers 12–21

Research checked 11 October 2026. This note describes source register and task framing observed in the local Cambridge 12–20 PDFs/OCR and, where noted, Cambridge 21 Ppo text. It is an editorial profile, not a lexical-frequency or readability study. The examples below are deliberately phrase-length; the corpus ledger paraphrases Task 2 prompts and does not reproduce passages or scripts.

## Listening: cooperative conversation, then organized information

Part 1 audioscripts tend to use ordinary conversational openings and polite requests around a concrete need: booking, joining a group, arranging a visit or comparing services. Short turns include contractions, acknowledgements, clarification, preferences and corrections. A small example from C20 Test 1, Part 1 is “I've been meaning to ask you” (script, printed pp. 147–148). C16 Test 1, Part 1 has the informal reaction “You'd be amazed” (audioscript, printed p. 98).

Part 2 changes to an announcement, guided explanation or service briefing. Part 3 uses student, trainee or project partners who negotiate a decision, check assumptions and sometimes revise a proposed answer. Part 4 is more continuous and topic-focused; the speaker organizes information through headings, stages, comparisons or examples. C12 Test 1 Part 4, for example, frames a talk as “Four business rules” (audioscript at the end of the test). The transitions are spoken and explicit, but the scripts do not use a formal lecture register throughout every part.

The scripts model concise, intelligible spoken interaction with some informal features. They are not a transcript of spontaneous conversation: answer-bearing details are deliberately placed near the question sequence, and distractors can be created by an initial suggestion, changed plan, or related but non-responsive detail. That describes observed item construction; it does not claim a fixed distractor quota.

## Reading: accessible nonfiction with local technical density

The passages are adapted public-facing texts across history, science, psychology, technology, ecology, culture and social questions. Explanatory pieces define unfamiliar concepts through examples or context. Argumentative pieces make claims, acknowledge alternatives and signal concession. Biographical and historical narratives give the account a clearer chronology. Texts generally use standard edited prose: topic sentences and paragraph links carry the line of reasoning, while a passage may retain a small number of discipline-specific terms.

The style combines direct exposition with attribution and qualified claims. A scientific passage in C13 Test 3, Passage 2 uses “Some research even suggests” (printed p. 64); a historical description in C15 Test 1, Passage 1 calls nutmeg a “highly prized and costly” ingredient (printed p. 15); a conservation passage in C20 Test 3, Passage 2 says “we also want to highlight” (PDF scan range pp. 79–115; printed footer pagination is unstable). These fragments show, respectively, cautious evidence framing, compact evaluative description and attributed advocacy. They do not establish a measured frequency of hedging or sentence length.

Passage density varies by genre and task. A short narrative account may proceed chronologically; a scientific explainer commonly moves from phenomenon to study or evidence; a policy argument may contrast an intervention with costs or limitations. Across the 40 papers, the topics remain accessible to a general reader, while the question tasks demand close distinctions between stated, contradicted and unstated information. Word-length figures remain layout estimates in [corpus.md](corpus.md), not measured counts.

## Writing: neutral task framing, explicit response obligations

Task 1 prompts use a neutral directive to summarize supplied visual information and report key features, often inviting relevant comparison. The visual, not outside knowledge, supplies the evidence. In C17 Test 4 the instruction includes “reporting the main features” (printed p. 93). The underlying response register is concise, factual and comparative; the prompt itself does not prescribe a sentence template or a fixed overview length.

Task 2 topics are drawn from general social, educational, environmental, technological and personal-life issues. Prompts state a situation or proposition, then specify an obligation: take a position, discuss two views, weigh advantages and disadvantages, evaluate a development, or address multiple questions. For example, C13 Test 1 asks “To what extent do you agree or disagree?” (printed p. 29). The question type should shape coverage of the response; the research does not reproduce the full prompt blocks. A writer model should preserve each obligation and allow more than one defensible position.

## Speaking: familiar personal answers broaden into discussion

The printed sample prompts move from familiar personal topics in Part 1 to a single sustained Part 2 topic with supporting points, then to Part 3 questions about wider social patterns. Cue cards are direct imperatives; Part 3 questions use ordinary interrogative wording and invite explanation, comparison, evaluation or prediction. A compact Part 2 fragment from C17 Test 4 is “something in a hurry” (printed p. 94); the related Part 3 frame is “Arriving late” (printed p. 95). C20 Test 1's cue card includes “see again with friends” (PDF scan range pp. 5–40; scan pages preserve the test, not reliable printed footers).

The books provide prompts, not a bank of candidate answers. Therefore this profile characterizes examiner-facing language and topic progression only; it does not infer how a Band 7 or Band 9 candidate would answer, or prescribe a memorized response shape. Cambridge 21 contributes no Speaking sample in the supplied corpus.

## Limits

No corpus-wide counts were made for lexical sophistication, hedging, sentence length, turn length or accent. Script text removes prosody, and the printed sources do not consistently identify speaker gender or accent. Passage genre labels and approximate lengths are stated in [corpus.md](corpus.md); unknown original source provenance is not filled in by guesswork. The language examples are not a substitute for expert review or learner trials.
